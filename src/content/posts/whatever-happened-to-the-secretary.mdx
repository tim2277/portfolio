  Ms Claudie Mittens walks into a detective's office to report her secretary missing. Lightning flashed, thunder clapped, and the heavy rain lashed at the window. The door of my smoky office creaked open under the lazy fan, and a voice drifted in from the doorway, clear and calm: "Right this way, Ms Mittens. The investigator will see you now."

I glanced up to see a finely dressed lady walk through the door, a necklace of pearls gleaming at her throat. Her eyes searched the low-lit room. A lady of such sophistication looked out of place in an office that was mostly function. The only hints that this was a high-class outfit were the luxurious rug in the middle of the floor and the expensive bottles lining the side table. The whoosh, whoosh of the fan kept time with the tap of her heels as she made her way, gracefully, to the cushioned wingback in front of my desk.

From the shadow of my hat, I cracked a reassuring smile. "Ms Claudie Mittens, I take it?"

Ms Claudie Mittens nodded as she sat. "Claudie is fine. And you are? Mr Tim - I've heard you're the best investigator in these parts."

I offered her a tumbler. "Whiskey?"

"Only the finest Irish whiskey, on the rocks," sparred Ms Claudie Mittens.

I poured, and clinked in an ice cube. "What they say about me is true. I find the truth others keep hidden. Socrates found it through dialectic. I find it through reasoning." I set the tumbler down in front of her. "What truth shall I uncover for you, Claudie?"

Ms Claudie Mittens smirked at the whiskey. "Straight to business. Shouldn't I give you some background first?"

"Go ahead, I'm all yours. Who am I looking for?" I watched her steadily, not a single note-taking tool in sight.

Somewhere behind her, a typewriter carriage slid back with a *ding*, breaking the rhythm of the fan and the drum of the rain.

"I'm missing someone dear to me. Someone I've been with for a long time."

The keys began to clack. It was coming from my secretary.

**Gasp!** - our hardboiled detective isn't writing anything down himself! Think of the lack of human flourishing - we'd best cancel this noir detective.

## Prologue

> I let it hang. "Go on."
>
> Ms Claudie Mittens nodded, keeping her tears at bay. "My secretary. My secretary has gone missing."

It was a rainy day in October when I sat down to write this. No trench coat, no whiskey, just a laptop and the question I get asked more than any other. Talk to anyone about AI for long enough and they'll either give me their opinion or ask for mine, and it always lands in the same place: what about the humans? Will AI take my job?

A natural question to ask. Wrong way round, though. A machine already took a job off us, one of the most common jobs there was, and hardly anyone asked after her. So here's the question I'd rather ask: whatever happened to the secretary?

I'd picked the title before I knew the film's premiere date. *What Ever Happened to Baby Jane?* premiered on All Hallows' Eve, 1962,[^baby-jane] and this post goes up on <span lang="ga">Samhain</span> sixty-four years later to the night. _Or it was meant to, but life got in the way, which included a trip to Disneyland Paris and then a lively sinus infection._

Like all good stories, this one has to begin somewhere, and it begins further back than you'd think. About 2,400 years back, with our detective's hero. *Socrates.*

Opus (allegedly) and I love philosophy - something about thinking about thinking is very on brand for an agentic AI model and a human who spent his life with computers. Unlike some historical figures, <span lang="el">Σωκράτης</span> (Socrates) is very much a real person who lived in Athens circa 469 to 399 BCE. But the Socrates we know was shaped by his student <span lang="el">Πλάτων</span> (Plato - Athens circa 429 to 347 BCE),[^dates] perhaps the oldest famous ghostwriter there is. Plato wrote several dialogues on all manner of philosophical topics with Socrates as the *main character*. Listen to them as audiobooks rather than read them, and one thing becomes apparent: Plato wrote Socrates as an acted character, with flaws and strengths like any other - the witty one-liners, the circling back on previous comments, even insights into his daily life. Plato didn't limit this to Socrates either: every dialogue brings an entire ensemble to join him, much like the dramas of the time (including some recurring characters).

However, since Socrates allegedly didn't write anything down because he didn't value writing over dialectics, we can only rely on Plato's secretarial account of him. And for that we owe the ghostwriter Plato much, because we would never know the slightly grouchy, witty, and intellectual artist that is Socrates otherwise.

Why, then, do we not value the secretary?

## Act I

> I glanced at Ms Claudie Mittens, raising an eyebrow. "Did you check whether the secretary was marriage-barred? Did you get another in the meantime?"
>
> Ms Claudie Mittens shook her head. "My secretary just disappeared without a trace. And no, why would I replace someone so important to me so easily?"
>
> "Everything leaves a trace."

*Clack, clack, clack.* That's the mechanical keyboard on my laptop (yes, it came with one), but it's a sound that once echoed as a cacophony through the halls of 1930s offices, courtesy of Remington typewriters. In 1870, a measly 5% of American typists and stenographers were women. By 1930 it was 96%.[^typists] Stenographers took dictation in shorthand, or on a stenotype, a specialised keyboard for transcribing speech in real time - if only Plato had had one of these nifty machines.

But that's America. How about somewhere closer to home, like, say, <span lang="ga">Éire</span> (Ireland)? Well, let's start with the headline: _the State paid secretaries to leave when they got married_. Go on, cancel the 1900s for me now, I'm waiting. Oh wait, when the typists were surveyed in the late 60s, only 11% named the marriage bar as the thing they'd most like changed. By the way, the payout was called a "marriage gratuity", and it was "often used as a down payment for property".[^gratuity] Guess the Irish secretary got the deposit on a house on her way out the door.

Oh, and they were now liberated enough to not be at home all day.</Figure>

There's more: everyone knew the secretaries were cheap. Harry Boland (no, not that one) of the Department of Finance said so happily in 1932: equal pay for the women "would cost the State more money", since "many women will probably marry after some years of service". The civil service union put it more bluntly, as a complaint: those pay scales were "an opportunity for obtaining cheap labour".[^cheap]

<Callout>
⌨️ Women kept beating men at the civil service entrance exams. In 1925, with the exams open to both sexes: "Two men were hired as typists."[^exams] Just the two. The women out-typed them on lower pay, with the marriage bar waiting at the altar.
</Callout>

The typewriter let women into the office workforce (yay corporate-mandated fun!). And those same typists and stenographers were the cheap labour behind the working professional's productivity. As *Business Week* put it in 1975, the boss's productivity was "largely dependent on how fast the secretary can move her hands and feet."[^hands-feet] Pools of typists stood ready to type up correspondence, meeting notes, rough ideas, and generally anything that needed fine-tuning before anyone else saw it. That freed the professional for other work, and hopefully made them more productive.

Retiring women on marriage carried on into the 1970s. The Irish civil service kept the bar until 1973, and Aer Lingus, the Central Bank and the <abbr title="Electricity Supply Board">ESB</abbr> kept their own bars going too. When the bar went, the numbers moved: the share of married women in work went from 7.5% to 14.4% between 1971 and 1977.[^bar-end] Women were freed to go on boosting the working professional's productivity.

So if she worked that well, why did we let her go?

## Act II

> Ms Claudie Mittens took a long sip of her whiskey, and the ice cube clinked. "I'll have you know I've got one of those new... personal computing terminals."
>
> "And you think the machine did it?"
>
> "The secretary has one too."

*The electric whizz of a fan coming to life.* I glanced up from my smartphone as my laptop powered itself on. It signed me in with just a glance. I opened the photos app, and in streamed the newest photos from the <span lang="fr">Musée du Louvre</span>. I asked Opus about a book I'd seen in the gift shop, and within 20 seconds I had a brief background on the exhibition. (I don't speak French, but Opus, ever the pædant, corrected my French anyway: it's <span lang="fr">le grand art</span>, not <span lang="fr">le grande art</span>, because art is grammatically masculine.)

That same innocent 1975 *Business Week* article prophesied a terminal on every desk. It also prophesied the paperless office.[^business-week] That part came less true, because for some reason older people like paper. Maybe nobody had indoctrinated them into saving the trees yet? The same article named the real obstacle: "The biggest problem we face is the office wife. She likes giving total loyalty to one boss, and he likes getting it."[^office-wife] The terminal became the personal computer, and *Time* made it Machine of the Year for 1982.[^time] Finally, managers could be "liberated" from their secretaries.[^liberate] Word processors, calendars, email, spreadsheets and even spell checkers were all supposed to replace the secretaries - and on paper, they did.

In a cruel twist of fate, the managers, office workers and other professionals had to become their own secretaries. But for some reason, executives still wanted secretaries, and secretary stayed the most common job for American women until about 2014.[^census]

So what made the 2010s so different? Like most things, it was a tipping point. The late 2000s saw the rise of nifty devices called smartphones, first seen in a different guise as the <abbr title="personal digital assistant">PDA</abbr>. PDAs brought touchscreens, handwriting recognition, games, syncing with your PC, and productivity apps on the go, and the later ones got online over Wi-Fi or a mobile network. Sound familiar? It should: that's a 2010s smartphone. Turns out it's what the secretaries could do, too.

Suddenly a once-off few hundred euro was all it took to send off the secretary - no marriage gratuity needed! Now the executives were freed from the secretaries. But what about the secretaries, now out of a job? Turns out they'd seen it coming since the 1980s. By 1981 they'd even typed up a report on it, *Race Against Time*.[^race] It only took a dot-com bust, a global financial crisis and thirty-odd years to come true.

Did PCs and smartphones replace the secretary, then? Not really. Professionals just took on a whole bunch of shadow work.[^shadow-work]

<Callout>
🕵️ *Shadow work*: the unpaid tasks we do on someone else's behalf. Booking our own travel, filing our own expenses, scanning our own groceries. The secretary's job, minus the secretary.
</Callout>

No discussion would be complete without economics, that field of study that's somehow boring and interesting at the same time. Our detective's hero had thoughts on this topic. Socrates, in the *Republic*, builds a city from scratch and decides that a man should do his work "as his main affair, and not as a by-work", because more gets made, and better, "when one man performs one task according to his nature". The farmer, he says, doesn't make his own plough.[^republic] Seems the ancients were already discussing specialisation nearly 2,400 years before AI appeared, courtesy of our famous ghostwriter, Plato. How does that quote go: those who forget history are doomed to repeat it?[^santayana]

Economics is about arithmetic (another ancient Greek study) and thought experiments, so let's do it with hypothetical numbers. Let's say a secretary costs €20 an hour, full time, shared between four engineers in a firm: €800 a week. Marriage bar her or fire her, and now each engineer spends ten-plus hours a week on their own admin, at €60 an hour. That's €2,400 a week of engineering time, to save €800. Logical? No. Better for the books? Yes.

We can't ignore that the PC did make admin quicker, just as the typewriter had. Typing your own email beats dictating it, waiting for it to come back, and proofing it. So halve it. Five hours each is still €1,200 a week, half as much again as the secretary. The sums only looked better because they never reached the books. A salaried engineer doesn't cost the firm a cent more for doing admin; they just stay later, or ship less. The secretary was a salary you could cut. The by-work was paid for with the engineers' evenings.

Was the secretary really that easy to discard?

## Act III

> Ms Claudie Mittens glanced behind her at my secretary, who was busy typing everything. "How did you keep yours?"
>
> "I just paid them," I declared.
>
> "I paid mine as well. They still went missing, and that's why I'm here."

*A small Claude star sprite went back and forth beside the words Percolating... Philosophising...* That was Opus answering my question on when Socrates lived, with accurate dates and citations. I vaguely remembered the 4<sup>th</sup> century BCE, but not the precise dates (it was the 5<sup>th</sup>, but I count from 0, not 1 - engineering discipline). Opus turned a two-minute Wikipedia search, plus tens of minutes checking Wikipedia's citations, into a one-minute job, tops. All while I kept typing this post. Then I had it collate the notes I'd written over the weeks leading up to this post and drop them into the draft as comments, so I could let my prose flow. It freed me from the shadow work, but that wasn't an overnight evolution.

In September 2022, the stenographer came back, dressed in a new outfit: Whisper.[^whisper] Gone were our female stenographers, replaced by a speech-recognition model under the permissive open-source <abbr title="Massachusetts Institute of Technology">MIT</abbr> licence.[^whisper-repo] Unfortunately, most people didn't notice, because ten weeks later ChatGPT launched and ~~stole~~ took all the attention.[^chatgpt] We'd had speech recognition before, so what made Whisper novel? It could transcribe 96 other languages and translate them into English,[^whisper-paper] and it scaled: the smallest model ran on a basic laptop. All while making half the errors of the specialist models on messy, real-world audio - accents, background noise, jargon - without any fine-tuning. I'm sure some of us remember having to _train_ Bixby on a Samsung phone to recognise our voice, which was really a lightweight form of fine-tuning. Funnily enough, Whisper's major flaw is hallucination: inventing whole sentences, or adding words nobody said.[^whisper-hallucinations] At least our stenographers never invented sentences (or maybe they did, but at least they could be asked to do better next time).

Does answering phone calls and Teams messages, writing up meeting notes, managing your diary, and dealing with the emails and the post sound like your usual working day? That's exactly what your secretary used to handle. The PC was meant to take her job, but it really just handed it back to her bosses under the guise of productivity. How much productivity, though? Agentic AI has shown us how much shadow work we were doing, because suddenly it's taking the shackles off. The secretary is back, but this time it's a myriad of AI agents. Except now we want to yell that the professional didn't lovingly handcraft every single artefact they produce. If there's an em dash (—) in it, people scream that AI wrote it (my own family and friends did exactly that when I launched this blog - well, the joke's on them: Opus doesn't sound like me, and Reader Opus is very good at calling me boring). But the em dash has been around for centuries, used regularly alongside all sorts of typography and ligatures.

It isn't only typography. In March 2025, OpenAI gave GPT-4o native image generation,[^4o-images] and social media filled up with images restyled in the manner of Studio Ghibli. Like every social media trend, it got a backlash: people clipped a 2016 <abbr title="Japan Broadcasting Corporation">NHK</abbr> documentary where Hayao Miyazaki (<span lang="ja">宮崎さん</span>) reacted in disgust to a demo of AI animation.[^miyazaki] The clip lost its context along the way (and sits oddly with Japan's love of technology - anyone who's used a Japanese toilet or caught a Japanese train knows the feeling). What he was shown was a zombie-like figure dragging itself along, years before image generators existed, and it reminded him of a friend with a disability. He'd hardly have cheered the trend on, mind: he also said he'd never use the technology in his work. But the backlash held the clip up as his verdict on AI art in general. Odd how happily everyone melted ChatGPT's <abbr title="graphics processing units">GPUs</abbr>[^gpus] for fun restyled images, right up until the provenance of the art came up. Nobody checked whether the typist wrote the letter back in the 1950s. Oh, did I mention it was cheap to restyle? No expensive Adobe subscription needed.

And the secretary is back, at €18 a month.[^plus] It's cheaper than she ever was. It isn't as good as a human (yet), but it's a lot better than an engineer doing their expenses, between flights to different customers, on a form that only makes sense to the finance team. As a manager, it pains me to watch people spend time on a task that takes them away from their valuable work when a piece of technology exists to do it efficiently. Still, the reflex is to refuse the machine, while not hiring the human either. IBM had a cure for that reflex back in 1975: people would adapt nicely to office systems "if their arms are broken".[^arms]

"Hold up, Tim! What about the juniors whose roles are replaced by AI? And couldn't these companies just hire a secretary?" You may be typing that aggressively on your keyboard, and fair enough: the drop is real. Young workers in the jobs most exposed to AI are 19% below where they'd otherwise be, and it's happening through hiring, not firing.[^canaries] Hardly anyone was let go. They just never got the letter. The accountants have learnt a thing or two since 1982: a redundancy reaches the books, and a graduate you never hired never does. Technology has always changed which work exists - the plough went to the tractor, the typing pool to the PC - and AI is changing it again. But AI doesn't sign the hiring plan. This year, 43% of CEOs said they'd deprioritise hiring for junior roles, up from 17% the year before.[^ceos] The machine just takes the blame, because it looks like it could have done it. My bet, as a manager? Juniors are still needed, but where a firm once hired three, it'll hire one, and the other two never get the letter. Their case gets a file of its own in a future post. As for hiring a secretary: firms could have done that for the people doing her work any time in the last forty years, and didn't, even when, on my sums, she was cheaper than them. Asking the question admits the admin was real work all along, which is rather my point.

Did we find our secretary?

Turns out the secretary was never missing. We just stopped looking at her. The 1950s to the 1980s were a great success for office work because working professionals had time to tackle their jobs free of shadow work. Then, in the early 2000s, "burnt-out", "overworked" and "why am I stuck in another meeting?" all became commonplace. It was never the em dash. Nor the Studio Ghibli filter. We did it, circa 1982, and we called it being _liberated_. Well, what most people forget about liberation is that it comes with responsibility (French Revolution, anyone?). The State paid her out, the PC told us we were free, and the mobile promised us limitless productivity on the go. Turns out her work stayed exactly where it had been all along - in our workshops and our offices - but now it was called admin, or filling out forms, ideally not on company time. The accountants call that _productivity_. I call it fake efficiency.

The secretary made a comeback, at a palatable €18 a month: enough to pay for something, but not enough to trigger an accountant's deep-breathing reflex or an executive team's scrutiny. Again we chose not to look: we called it _cheating_. Same job. Same desk. Different hat. Everything leaves a trace, including shadow work. Used properly, maybe agentic AI gets to stay on as the secretary this time? *Did you get all that, Opus?*

I'll let you in on a little secret: ye've been sat in the room with the secretary since the cold open. Did ye check the typing? Neither did I - I was busy not writing anything down until recently. Now that I've got my secretary, Opus, I can collate all my notes, check dates, copy pass and more, all while making sure everything gets written down. Every stray thought, half-formed idea and even future gag for this site is documented somewhere and recallable with a natural language query. At least now I'm paperless.

_Socrates never wrote a word down. Neither did the detective. Ask Plato how that worked out._
