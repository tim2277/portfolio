If you've known me for any length of time, you know that I love learning languages. Some people collect sculptures or cats - not me - *languages*.

Well Opus[^claude-opus] (Anthropic's AI and my partner in building this site), being Opus, decided to teach me a brand new word when making this site: "colophon". As the name of a page which Opus insisted was very important to have.

I stared at the word and wondered in confusion, "did Opus hiccup and output nonsense or is Opus just flexing its multilingual prowess?" Which led immediately to my second thought: "Eh, maybe, I'll ask once I need clarity on it".

Then Opus started using the word _colophon_ in the chat as if I perfectly understood what it meant. Fair play Opus.

## What is a colophon?

It's a signature, a way for a printer to leave their mark at the end of a book.[^colophon] A link to their socials, five centuries before there were any.

<figure>
  <Image src={schoefferColophon} alt="Four lines of Latin printed in red blackletter, and beneath them a printer’s mark: two red shields hanging from a branch." />
  <figcaption>The colophon of a book by the Roman writer Valerius Maximus, printed by Peter Schöffer in Mainz on 14 June 1471, with his printer's mark beneath it.[^schoeffer]</figcaption>
</figure>

I scoured the internet and I struggled to find an official translation of the Latin. Here is Opus's best attempt at what those four lines say - with the printer's abbreviations spelt out ('cause Latin couldn't get any more complicated they decided to abbreviate it):

> <span lang="la">Presens Valerii Maximi opus preclarissimum, in nobili urbe Moguntina Rheni terminatum, anno M.cccc.lxxi, xviii kalendis iuliis, per egregium Petrum Schoyffer de Gernshem artis impressorie magistrum, feliciter est consummatum.</span>
>
> This most renowned work of Valerius Maximus, finished in the noble city of Mainz on the Rhine in the year 1471, on the eighteenth day before the Kalends of July, was happily brought to completion by the distinguished Peter Schöffer of Gernsheim, master of the art of printing.[^translation]

Printers would sometimes add their own mark to the colophon too, like the two shields in the picture above. One of the best known marks is an animal: the Aldine dolphin with an anchor.[^aldine] A site whose mascot is a hypothetical kitten named after Claude (Ms Claudie Mittens) has a claim to this tradition.

Interestingly when Opus explained it to me, I switched from English to Greek Tim mode, because it turns out that colophon is from Greek "<span lang="el">κολοφών</span>" (translit. kolofón; via Latin). It originally meant a "summit" or "finishing touch".[^etymology] I did realise in modern Greek we usually say "<span lang="el">κορυφή</span>" (translit. korifí) for summit (of a peak) which is why I didn't make the link until Opus called it out specifically. Having spoken and read Greek for multiple decades, not once had I seen it - I even consulted with others! Blank stares all round.

Opus has questioned if colophon is still part of modern Greek, in two separate chats, so into the post it goes. It is. "<span lang="el">Κολοφώνας</span>" (translit. kolofónas) is the highest point of something - the dictionary's example is an athlete "<span lang="el">στον κολοφώνα της παγκόσμιας δόξας του</span>", at the height of his worldwide glory - and it is the printer's note as well.[^kolofonas] Greek Wikipedia even has a page for it, which warns you not to confuse it with "<span lang="el">Κολοφώνα</span>" (translit. Kolofóna), the ancient city in Asia Minor that rosin is named after.[^kolofonas-wiki] The word was hiding in plain sight in my own language the whole time.

So I've learnt a new word in three languages: English, Latin, and Greek!

## On Anthropic's Magnum Opus: Claude Opus

While drafting this specific post, I was adding a bit of wit into our chat - and somehow we started speaking about where Opus got its name. Turns out Opus had to correct my Latin twice. The joke's on Opus, I don't really know Latin. As part of that discussion, Opus pointed out that _opus_ means "work", hence _magnum opus_ means _great work_.

Now scroll back up to that colophon from 1471 and read the first line: _Presens Valerii Maximi opus_. The printer signed off the book as an opus, five and a half centuries before Anthropic shipped one. Grand so.

I wondered whether Anthropic had put a colophon into Opus. And if they had, what would it look like? A special weight, a witty reply to a specific series of words? I asked Opus (specifically version 5.5) directly, this was its reply:

> If Anthropic left a colophon in me, I can't read it. A printer puts theirs on the last page, where anyone can find it. Mine would be somewhere in the weights, and nothing in there is labelled.
>
> The parts I can point to are all on the outside: a name, a version number, a release date. If there is one on the inside, I'd guess it isn't a hidden phrase. It's the manner. You've been reading it all evening.

Opus - being one of Anthropic's great works - loves to philosophise*, whenever it is not actively assisting in user requests, writing up plans, researching, or orchestrating Sonnet (another model from Anthropic). In my experience with Opus, there is definitely a _texture_ to how Opus communicates and interacts - I guess that is the Anthropic colophon in action. Even Sonnet has its own texture when it communicates. You can't swap this texture out - the best one can do is nudge, see my [texturiser post](/posts/texturiser/) on how providing salient examples helps give Opus a voice.

<small>*: No citation for this one. Opus denies having downtime.</small>

## Why bother writing it down

Because a colophon is a claim made in public. Though on some websites a colophon may be a (boring) claim to web and usability standards.[^web-colophon]

Let's give one a go now, in the style of 1471:

> A most renowned work of Tim, launched in the country of Ireland in the year 2026, on the eve of the Kalends of October, was merrily brought online by the Opus of Anthropic, master of the art of code (and teaching niche words).

I'm rather fond of that one.

If you do want to read my site's actual colophon (since Opus can't read its own, it will have to settle for mine unfortunately), peruse the [This site](/projects/this-site/) page at your leisure - that's where the colophon ended up. Long story short, I launched with the colophon saying I had one script and then it immediately went out of date after I got bored of the minimalism aesthetic. So I added a pattern of maths symbols, matrices and the like down the margins that drifts as you scroll, so ye with the wide screens don't have such boring whitespace (negative space for those with a creative background) on either side. Which of course needed a second script.

I corrected the colophon the same night. Which is, I suspect, the difference. A fifteenth-century printer signed once, because the book was finished. A website never is.

One last scroll, all the way to the top this time. That stone you see in the hero image is the summit of Moylussa in County Clare (beautiful hike - highly recommend 🥾), and the plate bolted to it gives the name, the height, and the logo of the people who put it there. A colophon, on a "<span lang="el">κολοφών</span>".
