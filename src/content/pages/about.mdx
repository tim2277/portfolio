I started with science experiments in the backyard, which morphed into _pulling apart_ computers, then writing software for them. I eventually settled on making computers move and understand their environment through my Mechatronics[^mechatronics] and Robotics studies - including sending a wheeled robot full speed into walls or copying human movements on industrial robot arms. Eventually computers became smart enough that I could actually work with them in natural language and images, thus the MSc in AI. I've spent decades discovering why and how things work.

After years of "too much work", "who's going to maintain it" and "social media posts are boring", along came agentic dev[^agentic-dev] and I ran out of targets to blame.

## What I work on

Reasonably complex applications, mostly, in whatever language the job needs. I collect software languages (like I do human ones - more on that shortly). Though my favourite is C# (and the .NET platform[^dotnet] since 2005), maybe because it was my first? Let's not get started on C and C++ - always fun when the application crashes and the motor decides to get stuck on maximum throttle. My day job's focus is .NET Blazor[^blazor] and distributed applications.

What I keep coming back to is the seam between engineering practice, ISO standards (27001, looking at you)[^iso-27001], and tooling - the parts of delivery that quietly decide whether a team ships monthly or quarterly or not at all because a change breaks all access controls!

## Using AI to engineer (fun) stuff

Most of what I write about here. Not "AI will replace your job and everyone you love", but the more interesting and less glamorous question: where do agents actually fit into an existing <abbr title="Software Development Lifecycle">SDLC</abbr>?

That includes all the boring stuff like: reviewing reams and reams of code, triaging vague customer reproduction steps (none whatsoever), migrating to the latest tech, writing more reams of boring test code, keeping up all that documentation everyone says they'll do tomorrow but tomorrow never comes, and exploring what updates will break the code next.

But it also includes the fun stuff: my personal anecdotes, hints at what I do in my spare time, and all the things that didn't end up fun because something broke or wasn't possible _yet_.

This site is a fusion of the two, built with Anthropic's [Claude Opus](/posts/a-niche-word-courtesy-of-opus/)[^claude-opus], read aloud by an AI called Brian, and supervised by [Ms Claudie Mittens](/posts/texturiser/) who is a purely hypothetical kitten (maybe one day I'll have a real Ms Claudie Mittens).
